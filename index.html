3ReachThe model sets the step. The graders multiply the reach.100×truth

Unchecked, errors compound: at 1 bad hour in 20, half of all 14-hour tasks fail; that half-life, for a model alone, is its “time horizon”. Checks change the exponent: caught errors are redone, only misses add up, so checks missing 1 error in 100 give the same model 100 times the reach at the same odds. 6Best-of-NOne pick: grader truth beats attempts. Kept winners add up.2.1× / 2.9×select

The best of N attempts beats the average by i(N) standard deviations. Drawn from a normal curve, i(N) grows only like √(2 ln N), 1.54 at 10 and 3.24 at 1,000, so 100 times the attempts buy 2.1 times the gain; from a fat tail of index α it grows like N1/α: at α = 3 the best of 1,000 stands 8.02 out, and 100 times the attempts buy 5.5 times. Attempts sharing mistakes add little; a hundred copies of one idea count as one. At grader accuracy r the gain is r·i(N): on the normal curve, doubling accuracy is worth about 60 times the attempts at 10, 22,000 times at 100. Selection picks from the top of the grader's scores, where its errors gather: by 4,096 attempts unsound graders lost soundness, a shallow one from 0.94 to 0.32, and selection alone made a 0.53 hacking gap from honest attempts; one reward model's picks peaked at 2 attempts and lost 6 points by 32. This covers picking among rival attempts, not splitting the work (law 7).

Offers are picked differently: every winner above the hurdle keeps its budget (00 An offer), so different attempts add almost in proportion. With the 12,300 payment-verified startups TrustMRR showed on 8 Nov resampled as offers, keeping the top tenth held value per verdict steady from 10 offers to 10,000; keeping only the best cut it 6 to 10 times. So i(N) is read from each pool's measured tail, never assumed normal: the fatter the tail, the more each distinct attempt buys. 9Amdahl's lawThe serial part caps the parallel part.< 20×time

With a serial share s, N parallel workers speed up the whole by at most 1 ÷ (s + (1 − s) ÷ N). If a person holds 5% of the work, 1,000 agents go at most 19.6× faster than one; at 0.01%, 10,000 agents go about 5,000×. A practice made for a person's limits, such as one task at a time, a review queue or a working day, is a serial share too, even with no person in it.

One studio had AI answer its taste calls from two years of recorded decisions: in 8 days it decided 7,296 of 11,372 calls while its founder answered 27, and output rose from about 1,000 changes a day to 7,250 in one day. Anthropic's agents sat idle about 80% of their interactive session time, waiting for a person: at that share, 1,000 agents go 1.25× as fast as one. METR: with AI still mostly assisting people, Anthropic's progress on well-specified tasks ran about twice its old pace by October 2026, and automation that removes the human bottleneck could make it very rapid.

12TruthEach bit of truth a rival lacks doubles the lead.1 bit = 2×truth

Kelly (1956): one sure bit per even-money bet doubles the stake every bet, and over a noisy wire the fastest possible growth equals the information the wire carries. Information raises the growth rate by at most its mutual information with the outcome (Barron and Cover, 1988). In a horse race, splitting all one's money over the horses by the true odds grows fastest, and two bettors who split it by their own odds end one doubling apart for every bit by which one's odds beat the other's on the winners, by the log score, whatever the payouts: a wrong belief costs growth equal to its relative entropy from the truth (Cover and Thomas, 2006). Breiman (1961): sizing for the fastest growth ends ahead of every essentially different strategy and reaches a far goal soonest. A grade an agent writes itself is a function of what it already believes: it adds no information and buys no growth.

Evolution runs the same arithmetic: a cue raises a lineage's long-run growth rate by at most the mutual information between it and the environment, in many cases by exactly that (Donaldson-Matasci, Bergstrom and Lachmann, 2010). In the breeder's equation truth is accuracy r, the one factor that can turn negative: a grader that ranks backward breeds the wrong way, the faster for every added attempt, clone and generation. A verdict correlated r with true worth, both normal, holds −½·log₂(1 − r²) bits about it: 0.34 at r 0.61, 1.20 at 0.90.

A bit leads only while a rival lacks it. By Little's law, the average number of items in a system equals the rate they arrive times the average time each stays (Little, 1961). So the bits only one player holds equal, on average, the true bits it learns a day times the days each stays unseen: halve those days and they halve, as if it learned half as much.

So the engine counts its truth in bits: the graders seal its odds on every outcome before it settles, and its truth is the bits a day by which those odds beat the bare model's on what settles (05 Lead). It buys the cheapest true bits first, truer verdicts before more attempts (law 6), and counts a grade an agent can sway as none. It keeps each bit unseen as long as it can: it shows no rival a bit it could act on (03 Your story) and trades labs only blind scores for their next model first (03 Labs). Public models' lift is everyone's; the lead is the truth only the engine's record holds.

13GamePaid what they add, players' best moves are the genie's.2 → 0decay

In any game, the outcome that holds has each player's move a best reply to the others' (Nash, 1950). Pay each player what its move adds, the whole's gain with it minus without it, and serving the whole becomes its best move, whatever the others do (Vickrey, 1961; Clarke, 1971; Groves, 1973). Where players gain together, the one fair split, paying nothing for adding nothing, additive across games and paying out exactly the whole, gives each its average gain over every order of joining (Shapley, 1953). In a population, a trait spreads by its covariance with what is rewarded (Price, 1970): whatever a score pays that true gain does not spreads too. LLM agents reported true preferences substantially more often than people under deferred acceptance, a matching rule where the truth is every proposer's best move (June 2026). Outside, two sellers of the same thing, neither short of capacity, price it at cost (Bertrand, 1883); a claim informs only where faking it costs more than it brings (Spence, 1973); a commitment persuades when it cannot be undone (Schelling, 1960); what others' experiments show is free to whoever sees it (Bolton and Harris, 1999).

So inside, each player earns place, budget or share only for what it adds, read where it cannot reach; each model's best reply to a rule is measured, never assumed. Outside, the engine buys labs' next models first with scores no one else can give; sells where rivals cannot match its cost or copy its proof; never agrees with a rival on prices or markets; signals only with what is costly to fake; keeps its promises in code; takes every rival's verdict, never showing which of its offers or markets win (03 The game). Rivals' moves show in the decay term, read from winners' own profit (03 Decay).

Running

Some part can move, even as its checks change or you answer.

Waiting

No part can move until a grader decides, you give a key or approval (04), a plan's logins return from their limit (07 Plans) or an ended model's runner-up passes its test (Events). Done

Its finish passes every hidden check and the result ships its proof. A production result stays live until you stop it or its offer retires (Standing wish), its hidden checks rerunning against the world (Events).

To Running when a revert reopens it or the world or a new check breaks it. To Queued when your answer differs from a best guess it rests on; when you say no to its going-out item, that no becoming a check of what must not happen; or when you reject it, its live copy back to what was live before, else offline (04 Setup run).

Failed

Its own budget is spent, a grader the agents cannot reach upholds its lead's proof that it is impossible, or a wait on you or a grader's fix passed its deadline. It keeps what it learned and landed; its switch goes off; its live copy returns to what was live before, else offline (04 Setup run).

Stopped

You stopped it or everything (00); it runs as two regions. Work stops: nothing of it runs, spends, launches or goes out; checkout and renewals pause. Promises run on, first in line for money as it comes, none set aside (07 Money): each paid step's refund once its deadline passes, refunds buyers ask for or the rails flag (Tolerance) within Stripe's caps, and answers to disputes. A restart first reruns its live results' hidden checks, then returns it to the state it left, or to Running where one fails.

The breeder's equation

The expected best of N independent attempts is the mean plus r·σ·i(N), i(N) the expected maximum of N draws of unit variance: from a normal curve 0.56, 1.54, 2.51, 3.24, 3.85 for 2 to 10,000, growing like √(2 ln N); from a Student t of α degrees of freedom, tail index α, 1.46, 8.02 and 17.37 at 10, 1,000 and 10,000 for α = 3, growing like N1/α. Lush, 1937: R = h²S; Rendel and Robertson, 1950: gain a year divides it by the generation interval. The hero's simulation keeps only a generation's best measured change, moving h² of the way to it; the genie keeps every winner that passes, each at its own verdict (07 Kept).

The reach, on paper

A run of L hours at q errors an hour, a share m of which every check misses, is right with odds exp(−q·m·L); at fixed odds L grows as 1 ÷ (q·m), so halving misses or errors doubles the reach, and a work year (50 work weeks) needs checks 50 times stricter than a work week. Unchecked at q = 1 in 20, half of 14-hour runs fail (ln 2 ÷ 0.05 = 13.9 h); checks missing 1 in 100 stretch that to 1,386 hours. Layered checks multiply misses only where blind spots differ: m = s + (1 − s)·m₁·m₂·…, s the share no layer sees.
